Law firm websites and Google’s “Your Money or Your Life” content category

Over the past few weeks, many law firms have seen noticeable drops in organic search traffic following Google’s latest algorithm updates.

For some firms, rankings that stayed stable for years have suddenly slipped. Enquiry volumes have slowed. Practice area pages that once performed consistently are no longer attracting the same visibility.

The reason is not random.

Law firm websites sit within one of Google’s most heavily scrutinised content categories: YMYL, short for “Your Money or Your Life”. In simple terms, Google applies far stricter quality standards to legal content because inaccurate information can directly affect someone’s finances, legal rights, personal wellbeing, or future decisions.

As AI becomes more deeply integrated into search results, Google must place greater trust in the content it surfaces. That shift is changing the standards law firm websites are judged against.

As our CEO Stephen Moore recently discussed on LinkedIn after reports emerged of law firms losing organic traffic following recent updates:

“Old content that cut the mustard for many years may not be good enough any more.”

Stephen Moore, CEO, MLT Digital

That observation reflects a much wider shift taking place across legal search.

This article explores what YMYL means for law firms, why older SEO tactics are becoming less effective, and what firms should do now to maintain visibility in an AI-driven search environment.

What does “Your Money or Your Life” mean?

Many firms have heard the term YMYL without fully understanding the commercial implications.

Google uses the YMYL classification for websites covering subjects that can significantly affect a person’s life, finances, health, safety, or legal position. Legal websites fall directly into this category.

The reason is straightforward. Someone relying on inaccurate legal information could make decisions with serious consequences. Search engines therefore apply a much higher threshold for trust, credibility, and authority.

Google’s own YMYL guidance on creating helpful content makes this position clear. The search engine increasingly prioritises:

Demonstrable expertise
Accurate and trustworthy information
Clear authorship
Helpful user experiences
Up-to-date content
Genuine authority signals

This is closely linked to Google’s E-E-A-T framework:

E
Experience
E
Expertise
A
Authoritativeness
T
Trustworthiness

For law firms, those signals are no longer optional additions to a marketing strategy. They now sit at the centre of sustainable search visibility.

Why law firms are seeing traffic drops now

Many firms assume ranking declines must mean a penalty. In most cases, that is not what is happening.

The issue is that Google’s standards for evaluating legal content are evolving rapidly.

Search engines are increasingly using AI-generated summaries and conversational search experiences. Google now attempts to answer many queries directly within search results instead of simply displaying a list of blue links.

That changes the way content is assessed.

Google needs confidence that the information it surfaces is:

Accurate Reliable Current Clear Consistent Produced by credible sources

Content marketing that performed well for years may now appear:

Thin Generic Outdated Over-optimised Insufficiently authoritative Lacking real-world credibility

This is one reason many older law firm websites are struggling after recent updates.

Traditional SEO tactics often focused heavily on keywords, page volume, and technical optimisation. Those elements still matter, though they are no longer enough on their own.

Trust has become measurable.

Authority is now assessed not only at page level but across the entire website and brand.

The AI shift: why search is changing faster than many firms realise

AI is changing more than rankings. It is reshaping how information is delivered altogether.

Google’s AI Overviews and conversational search tools increasingly summarise answers directly within search results. Users often receive legal information before clicking through to a website.

That means search engines must evaluate content with far greater scrutiny.

AI systems need confidence in:

Expertise Accuracy Source reputation Topical authority Consistency Structured information

This is not simply another algorithm adjustment. It represents a structural shift in how legal information is evaluated online.

Firms relying on outdated content strategies may find their visibility reduced even if they previously ranked well for years.

The law firms gaining traction are often those producing:

Clear legal guidance
Well-structured content
Strong authorship signals
Consistent topic coverage
Demonstrable expertise
Helpful user experiences

AI-driven search environments reward confidence and credibility.

What Google appears to reward more heavily

Recent updates suggest Google is placing increasing emphasis on several trust-related signals.

These include:

Genuine Expertise

Content written or reviewed by experienced legal professionals carries greater credibility.

Clear Authorship

Named lawyers, detailed biographies, qualifications, and professional credentials help strengthen trust.

Updated Content

Regularly reviewed legal information appears more reliable than pages left untouched for years.

Helpful User Experience

Clear navigation, accessible layouts, and logically structured pages improve usability.

Topical Depth

Comprehensive coverage of legal subjects often performs better than shallow standalone pages.

Consistency Across Content

Websites with aligned messaging, terminology, and authority signals across all pages appear stronger.

Real-World Credibility

Awards, case studies, reviews, media mentions, and external references can all strengthen trust.

Generic SEO copy is becoming increasingly ineffective within YMYL sectors.

Google appears far more focused on whether a firm genuinely demonstrates expertise rather than simply targeting keywords.

Common weaknesses on law firm websites

Many firms are not losing visibility because they recently made mistakes.

The wider search environment has evolved around them.

Common weaknesses now affecting legal websites include:

Thin Service Pages

Short pages with limited legal insight often struggle to demonstrate authority.

Duplicate Practice Area Content

Repetitive content across multiple location pages weakens credibility.

No Named Authors

Anonymous content provides fewer trust signals.

Outdated Blogs

Old articles containing outdated legal references may reduce perceived reliability.

Over-Optimised Keywords

Content written primarily for search engines rather than users often feels unnatural.

Weak E-E-A-T Signals

Limited evidence of expertise or trust can reduce visibility.

Poor Internal Linking

Disconnected content weakens topical authority.

No Wider Content Strategy

Publishing isolated pages without broader subject coverage limits authority development.

AI-Generated Content Without Oversight

Poorly reviewed AI copy can introduce inaccuracies and weaken credibility.

Many firms still rely on tactics that worked effectively several years ago. Search expectations have changed significantly since then.
